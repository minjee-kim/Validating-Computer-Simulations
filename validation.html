Validation

Purpose

Working notes on what validation means across simulation and computer-model literatures.

For each paper:

  • What problem is being solved?
  • What does “validation” mean?
  • What real-world data are used?
  • How is the simulator compared with reality?
  • How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?
  • How is model adequacy decided?
  • What limitations or open problems remain?
  • What should be read next?

Literature Review

1. Collins, Koehler & Lynch (2024)

What problem is this paper solving?

  • There is no universally accepted procedure for validating an agent-based model.
  • ABMs are especially difficult to validate because both their mechanisms and the systems they represent can be complex.
  • The paper organizes validation-supporting methods and provides practical guidance for choosing among them.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • Validation asks whether a model adequately represents the system under study for its intended purpose.
  • Validation is therefore purpose-dependent rather than a claim that a model is universally “correct.”
  • Validation is also closely connected to model credibility with users and stakeholders.

What data from reality are used?

  • This is a review, not a validation study of one dataset.
  • The empirical-validation methods discussed can use social-media data, surveys, demographic and spatial data, observational field studies, historical data, or other measurements of the system being modeled.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • The paper reviews nine validation-supporting approaches:
    • data analytics,
    • docking,
    • empirical validation,
    • sampling,
    • visualization,
    • bootstrapping,
    • causal analysis,
    • inverse generative social science,
    • role-playing.
  • Empirical validation can directly compare simulated and empirical outputs, including distributions and other summary features.
  • The authors recommend using multiple complementary validation methods rather than treating validation as a single statistical test.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • Sampling and sensitivity analysis are used to study how uncertainty in inputs affects outputs.
  • Replication and adequate sample sizes are important when simulation output is stochastic.
  • Bootstrapping is discussed as a possible tool when real-world data are limited.
  • The paper does not provide one unified probabilistic model for all sources of uncertainty.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • There is no universal cutoff.
  • Adequacy depends on the model’s intended purpose, application domain, available data, and what validation evidence is considered credible by relevant stakeholders.
  • The authors recommend at least two different types of validation-supporting methods when possible.
  • When no discipline-specific requirements exist, they suggest empirical validation plus face validation as a useful starting combination.

Limitations / open problems

  • The paper gives guidance rather than a formal algorithm for selecting validation procedures.
  • Validation remains discipline-dependent.
  • No single set of methods guarantees validity.
  • The review does not solve how much simulation is enough or when a validation exercise should stop.

Relevant references to follow

  • Sargent (2013), Verification and Validation of Simulation Models.
  • Windrum, Fagiolo & Moneta (2007), Empirical Validation of Agent-Based Models.
  • Klügl (2008), A Validation Methodology for Agent-Based Simulations.
  • Balci (1998), Verification, Validation, and Accreditation.
  • Grimm et al. (2020), work on agent-based model development and validation.

2. Sargent (2013)

What problem is this paper solving?

  • Simulation models are increasingly used for decisions, so users need evidence that the implementation is correct and that the model is sufficiently accurate for its intended use.
  • The paper provides a general framework for verification and validation, validation techniques, documentation, and decisions about model validity.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • A model is valid when its output behavior has the accuracy required for its intended purpose over its intended domain of application.
  • The paper separates:
    • data validity,
    • conceptual model validity,
    • computerized model verification,
    • operational validity.
  • Verification asks whether the conceptual model was implemented correctly; validation asks whether the resulting model is sufficiently representative of the system for the intended application.

What data from reality are used?

  • This is a general methodological paper rather than one empirical application.
  • When the real system is observable, system input/output behavior data are used.
  • Data are needed for model construction, model evaluation, and simulation experiments.
  • Sargent stresses that inadequate real-world data can make strong operational validation impossible.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • Operational validation compares model and system behavior under multiple experimental conditions.
  • Common comparisons include:
    • graphical comparisons,
    • confidence intervals,
    • hypothesis tests.
  • Comparisons may involve means, variances, distributions, or time-series behavior.
  • When the real system is not observable, model behavior may instead be explored or compared with other models.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests quantify uncertainty in comparisons.
  • Sensitivity analysis is used to explore model behavior.
  • Sample size affects the probability of accepting or rejecting a model.
  • Sargent distinguishes:
    • model builder’s risk: rejecting an adequate model,
    • model user’s risk: accepting an inadequate model.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • The required accuracy should be defined before or very early in model development.
  • Adequacy is judged relative to that required accuracy and the intended application.
  • The paper does not propose one universal validity test.
  • Validation may be decided by the development team, users/sponsors, or an independent verification and validation process.

Limitations / open problems

  • What counts as “acceptable accuracy” remains application-specific.
  • There is no universal algorithm for deciding which validation techniques should be used.
  • Strong validation may be impossible when system data are unavailable.
  • The framework predates many modern problems involving high-dimensional stochastic simulators, emulators, and expensive simulation budgets.

Relevant references to follow

  • Naylor & Finger (1967), early simulation verification/validation work.
  • Balci & Sargent (1981), statistical validation and model-user/model-builder risk.
  • Balci (1998), verification, validation, and accreditation.
  • Kleijnen (1999), statistical techniques and data availability for model validation.

3. Bayarri et al. (2007)

What problem is this paper solving?

  • The central question is: Does the computer model adequately represent reality?
  • The paper develops a formal statistical workflow for computer-model validation that incorporates multiple sources of uncertainty and allows predictions in settings without direct field observations.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • Validation is an assessment of whether computer-model predictions represent reality adequately for the quantities and uses of interest.
  • The emphasis is not simply on whether model and field observations are equal.
  • Instead, the framework quantifies systematic model bias/discrepancy and uncertainty in predictions.

What data from reality are used?

  • The published Technometrics paper uses a resistance spot-welding test bed to illustrate the framework.
  • Field/physical measurements are combined with runs from the computer model.
  • Earlier versions of the framework also used automotive examples.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • The framework has six iterative steps:
    1. specify model inputs and their uncertainty/ranges using an Input/Uncertainty map;
    2. define evaluation criteria;
    3. collect field data and design computer experiments;
    4. approximate expensive computer-model output;
    5. combine computer runs and field observations to estimate calibration parameters, bias/discrepancy, and predictive uncertainty;
    6. feed results back into further experiments or model revision.
  • The computer model can be approximated with a Gaussian-process response surface when direct evaluation is expensive.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • The Bayesian framework combines uncertainty from:
    • unknown/tuning inputs,
    • field measurement error,
    • limited computer runs,
    • emulator uncertainty,
    • model discrepancy/bias.
  • Posterior prediction and uncertainty bounds are central outputs.
  • The original framework is primarily oriented toward deterministic computer codes rather than intrinsically stochastic simulators.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • The framework estimates discrepancy and predictive error with uncertainty bounds.
  • Adequacy is evaluated relative to predefined evaluation criteria and the precision required for the model’s intended application.
  • This makes validity more useful as a statement about predictive accuracy for a specific purpose than as a universal valid/invalid label.

Limitations / open problems

  • Calibration parameters and model discrepancy can be strongly confounded.
  • Results depend on assumptions used for the emulator and discrepancy process.
  • Physical field data are required for direct validation.
  • The original framework does not directly solve replication allocation for stochastic simulators.
  • Computational cost can still be substantial for large or high-dimensional models.

Relevant references to follow

  • Kennedy & O’Hagan (2001), Bayesian computer-model calibration.
  • Bayarri et al. (2007), Computer Model Validation With Functional Output.
  • Higdon et al., work combining computer simulations and field observations.
  • Later work on calibration/discrepancy identifiability.

4. Kennedy & O’Hagan (2001)

Citation

  • Kennedy, M. C., & O’Hagan, A. (2001). Bayesian Calibration of Computer Models. Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B, 63(3), 425–464. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9868.00294

What problem is this paper solving?

  • Complex computer codes often contain unknown calibration inputs.
  • Traditional calibration finds values that make simulator output close to observations and then treats those fitted values as fixed.
  • The paper develops a Bayesian approach that propagates remaining calibration uncertainty and explicitly allows the simulator to be structurally imperfect.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • This is primarily a calibration and prediction paper, not a validation framework.
  • Its importance for validation is the distinction between:
    • uncertainty about the correct input parameters, and
    • model discrepancy, meaning systematic differences between the best simulator output and reality.
  • A good parameter fit does not imply that the model itself is correct.

What data from reality are used?

  • The main real-data example is the 1993 Tomsk-7 chemical-plant accident.
  • The analysis uses an aerial survey containing 695 measurements of ruthenium-106 deposition.
  • The paper also considers a more complex simulated nuclear-accident exercise.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • Field observations are modeled using:
    • the computer-model response at unknown calibration parameters,
    • a discrepancy function between the computer model and reality,
    • observation error.
  • Gaussian processes are used to represent the computer response and/or discrepancy.
  • In the Tomsk analysis, subsets of the field observations are used for calibration and predictions are evaluated against remaining observations.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • The posterior distribution propagates:
    • calibration-parameter uncertainty,
    • emulator uncertainty,
    • model discrepancy,
    • observational error,
    • uncertainty in Gaussian-process hyperparameters.
  • The underlying computer model is treated as deterministic; stochastic simulator variability is not the main problem considered.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • The paper emphasizes predictive distributions rather than a binary validation decision.
  • Predictive performance can be examined using held-out physical observations and prediction error.
  • Correcting for model discrepancy improves prediction when the simulator is systematically imperfect.

Limitations / open problems

  • Calibration parameters and discrepancy are not generally identifiable from field data alone.
  • Different combinations of calibration parameters and discrepancy can explain the same observations.
  • The authors caution against interpreting calibrated parameters as the true physical parameter values.
  • High-dimensional calibration remains difficult.
  • Results can depend on Gaussian-process covariance assumptions.
  • The framework was designed around deterministic computer codes.

Relevant references to follow

  • Bayarri et al. (2007), validation framework.
  • Brynjarsdóttir & O’Hagan (2014), importance of model discrepancy for learning physical parameters.
  • Tuo & Wu (2015/2016), identifiability and consistency in computer-model calibration.
  • Modern reviews of computer-model calibration and discrepancy.

5. Windrum, Fagiolo & Moneta (2007)

What problem is this paper solving?

  • Agent-based models often contain many parameters and can produce many possible simulated “worlds.”
  • The paper asks how ABMs should be empirically connected to the real systems they claim to explain.
  • It compares three approaches to empirical validation:
    • indirect calibration,
    • the Werker-Brenner approach,
    • history-friendly modeling.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • Empirical validation evaluates how well the model’s data-generating process represents the real-world data-generating process.
  • A model should reproduce and, ideally, explain empirically observed stylized facts rather than merely generate plausible-looking trajectories.

What data from reality are used?

  • This is a methodological review rather than one application.
  • The approaches use combinations of:
    • micro- and macro-level empirical datasets,
    • panel/time-series data,
    • stylized facts,
    • historical knowledge,
    • detailed industry histories and case studies.
  • Examples include labor-market relationships such as the Beveridge curve and historical evolution of the computer and pharmaceutical industries.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • Indirect calibration:
    1. identify stylized facts;
    2. ground the model’s micro-level behavior in empirical evidence;
    3. finely sample the parameter space and retain parameter combinations capable of reproducing the observed facts;
    4. use the retained region to investigate mechanisms and new empirical implications.
  • At each parameter point, Monte Carlo simulation can generate a distribution for the statistic of interest and test whether the observed statistic could plausibly have arisen from the model.
  • Werker-Brenner uses empirical evidence to constrain initial conditions and parameter ranges before output validation.
  • History-friendly models calibrate mechanisms and parameters to a specific historical case and compare simulated histories with the observed history.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • Stochastic variability is explicitly recognized as a major issue.
  • The indirect-calibration approach uses repeated Monte Carlo simulation to characterize distributions of output statistics for a given parameter setting.
  • Sensitivity analysis is treated as important for understanding whether conclusions are robust.
  • The authors criticize history-friendly work for insufficient treatment of cross-run stochastic variability.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • There is no universal rule.
  • In indirect calibration, a parameter configuration may be retained when the model does not reject the empirically observed stylized fact at a chosen confidence level.
  • In history-friendly modeling, a “good” model is expected to reproduce multiple observed empirical patterns or historical features.

Limitations / open problems

  • Over-parameterization: models may have enough freedom to reproduce broad empirical patterns almost regardless of mechanism.
  • Multiple possible worlds: many different parameter combinations may remain compatible with the same empirical facts.
  • Different parameter configurations can generate the same or very similar output traces.
  • It can be unclear whether an observed historical trace is typical of the real data-generating process.
  • Stylized facts may hide important dynamic information.
  • Counterfactual interpretations are difficult in stochastic, non-ergodic systems.
  • Empirical information may be incomplete or contradictory.
  • The relationship between validation results and policy conclusions remains unresolved.

Relevant references to follow

  • Werker & Brenner (2004), empirical calibration.
  • Fagiolo & Dosi and related work on indirect calibration.
  • Malerba et al. (1999), history-friendly modeling of the computer industry.
  • Malerba & Orsenigo (2001), pharmaceutical-industry history-friendly model.
  • Moss (2008), alternative approaches to empirical validation of ABMs.

6. Belfrage et al. (2024)

What problem is this paper solving?

  • ABMs are increasingly proposed for policy decisions, but verification and validation are often poorly performed or documented.
  • Public organizations also lack established procedures for deciding whether an ABM is credible enough to use for policy.
  • The paper develops a V&V planning approach and proposes an accreditation framework for policy-facing ABMs.

What does “validation” mean here?

  • Validation concerns the alignment between the model and the target system for the intended application.
  • The authors distinguish validity from credibility.
  • They define model credibility as a measure of confidence in the model’s inferential capability.
  • A valid model may still fail to inspire credibility, while perceived credibility without sufficient validity creates model-user risk.

What data from reality are used?

  • This is mainly a conceptual/framework paper rather than an empirical validation of one ABM.
  • The paper reviews existing V&V practices and uses the U.S. Coast Guard accreditation process as an institutional example.
  • It provides an illustrative COVID-19 school-opening policy scenario.
  • The authors explicitly leave real-world validation of their proposed accreditation framework for future work.

How is the simulator compared with reality?

  • The proposed V&V plan can include:
    • conceptual-model validation,
    • robustness analysis,
    • statistical comparison of simulation outputs with empirical data,
    • uncertainty and sensitivity analysis,
    • model-dynamics validation,
    • expert assessment.
  • The accreditation process specifies the intended use and acceptance criteria before deciding whether the model is fit to support a policy decision.
  • Post-implementation data should be used to evaluate both the policy and model after application.

How are uncertainty and stochasticity handled?

  • The paper stresses uncertainty from model inputs, the model itself, empirical referents, and social-system behavior.
  • Quantitative uncertainty should be measured where possible.
  • Non-quantifiable uncertainty should be communicated qualitatively and contextually.
  • Sensitivity and robustness analyses are important because ABM conclusions may depend on assumptions and path-dependent dynamics.
  • The paper notes the usefulness of separating fitting and testing data to reduce overfitting.

How do they decide the model is adequate?

  • The goal is not to prove that a model is “correct.”
  • An independent accreditation process should determine whether the available V&V evidence supports the model’s inferential capability for a specific intended application.
  • Acceptance criteria should reflect the importance, risk, and intended use of the model.
  • Models may be accredited with explicit limitations rather than accepted or rejected globally.

Limitations / open problems

  • The proposed accreditation framework is stylized and has not yet been validated through a real-world application.
  • Accreditation systems developed for military or physical simulations may not transfer directly to uncertain social systems.
  • Formal methods for robustness assessment remain limited.
  • Comprehensive V&V and accreditation can be resource-intensive.
  • Suitable acceptance criteria for policy-facing ABMs remain application-specific.

Relevant references to follow

  • Balci (1994/1998), model credibility and VV&A.
  • Law, Simulation Modeling and Analysis.
  • Duong (2010), accreditation and ABMs.
  • Collins, Koehler & Lynch (2024), ABM validation methods.
  • European Commission / Joint Research Centre guidance on models for policy.
  • U.S. Coast Guard documentation on simulation accreditation.

Takeaways

  • Validity is purpose-specific. None of these papers support a useful universal statement that a simulator is simply “valid.”

  • Calibration is not validation. Matching observed outcomes by tuning parameters does not by itself establish model adequacy.

  • Parameter non-uniqueness is central. Different parameter combinations may produce the same or similar emergent behavior.

  • Real-world comparison matters, but data may be sparse. This motivates uncertainty quantification, multiple forms of validation evidence, and careful separation of fitting from testing.

  • Stochasticity needs explicit treatment. Repeated simulation, Monte Carlo distributions, sensitivity analysis, and uncertainty decomposition appear repeatedly.

  • There is no universal stopping rule. The literature gives procedures for validation, but the question of when enough simulation evidence has accumulated remains important for this project.
